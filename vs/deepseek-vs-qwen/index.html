DeepSeek vs Qwen: open model families compared

Matt Kerr · Outlier · published 2026-07-23 Updated 2026-07-23
Quick answer

DeepSeek and Qwen both publish real open weights under permissive licenses — DeepSeek's V4 models are MIT, every Qwen open-weight model is Apache 2.0 — so both are safe to run, fine-tune and redistribute commercially with no gated request form. The practical difference is size: DeepSeek's open line starts at 284B parameters and tops out at 1.6T, while Qwen ships eight open sizes from 0.8B to 397B. If the question is what runs on your own Mac, the answer is Qwen, not DeepSeek.

Most open-model comparisons stop at benchmark charts. The questions that decide things are duller: what license are the weights under, which sizes exist, and will any of them fit on the machine in front of you? DeepSeek and Qwen answer those three very differently, though both are Chinese-developed families that publish downloadable checkpoints while also selling hosted access to models they never release.

Disclosure, because it matters: I build Outlier, a Mac app that runs models on-device, and six of its seven tiers are Qwen weights — the seventh is Gemma. I'm not a neutral party on this axis. Read the Qwen-favorable parts accordingly; I've tried to let the sourced numbers do the work.

The size ladders aren't comparable

DeepSeek's open V4 line is two checkpoints, both on Hugging Face under the MIT License. DeepSeek-V4-Flash is 284B total parameters with 13B activated per token, mixed FP4/FP8 precision with the MoE experts in FP4, and 1M token context. DeepSeek-V4-Pro is 1.6T total with 49B activated, also 1M context. Real weights, no legal strings — and also datacenter artifacts.

Qwen's open line is a ladder, shipped in four waves: Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (Feb 16, 2026), the mid sizes 122B-A10B, 35B-A3B and 27B (Feb 24), the small sizes 9B, 4B, 2B and 0.8B (Mar 2), then Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Apr 16) and Qwen3.6-27B (Apr 22). That last one is dense, 64 layers on a hybrid Gated DeltaNet plus Gated Attention design, with 262,144 tokens of native context extensible to roughly 1,010,000 via YaRN. Alibaba's repo pitches it as "Flagship-Level Coding in a 27B Dense Model."

The catch: Qwen's strongest models aren't in the open line. qwen3.7-max, qwen3.7-plus and qwen3.8-max-preview are API-only, so the open family visibly trails the proprietary flagship.

Licensing decides what you can build

This is under-covered, and it determines whether a product like mine can legally exist. DeepSeek's V4 repos state the weights are under the MIT License. Qwen states all its open-weight models are Apache 2.0. Both are OSI-approved, both permit commercial use, redistribution and fine-tuning without asking anyone, and Apache 2.0 adds an explicit patent grant that MIT leaves implicit.

Compare the common alternatives. Meta's Llama Community License and Google's Gemma Terms of Use aren't open source licenses in the OSI sense — they're custom vendor licenses attaching conditions Apache 2.0 and MIT don't, including acceptable-use policies that travel downstream to whoever you redistribute to. I ship one Gemma-based tier, so I live with it: the terms get passed along, and I track a policy a third party can revise. Read the actual text yourself — I'm describing the shape of the obligations, not quoting them. For a downstream product, MIT and Apache 2.0 are the difference between "publish it" and "get a lawyer first."

What runs on a Mac, and at what RAM

Here I can give measured numbers, because these are the checkpoints Outlier ships. Quantized, on Apple Silicon: Qwen3.5-4B is a 2.37 GB download needing 6 GB of RAM, at 71.7 tok/s on an M1 Ultra and about 32 tok/s on an M4 MacBook Air. Qwen3.5-9B is 5.04 GB and 12 GB RAM at 53.4 tok/s. Qwen3.6-27B is 15.13 GB and wants 24 GB, measured at 20.7 tok/s. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is 19.0 GB and 24 GB RAM. Qwen3.5-397B-A17B is a 209 GB download needing 64 GB, streaming experts from SSD with paged MoE at roughly 11 GB peak RSS and 2.1 tok/s.

Neither DeepSeek V4 checkpoint has a Mac story. 284B in mixed FP4/FP8 is already past what 64 GB holds. Sparse MoE helps, but the weights still have to live somewhere. On a Mac, DeepSeek means the browser and their API.

Strengths by task, and language coverage

DeepSeek's clearest strength is hosted cost. v4-flash at $0.14 per million input tokens is far below Western providers, and a cache hit drops that to $0.0028 — roughly fifty times cheaper than a miss. The counterweight is churn: DeepSeek's docs announce that the widely-integrated deepseek-chat and deepseek-reasoner names are deprecated as of 2026/07/24 15:59 UTC, and reserve the right to adjust prices.

Qwen's strength is breadth. Beyond text: Qwen3-VL for vision, Qwen3-Omni for text/audio/image/video in and text/audio out, plus ASR, TTS, embedding and image generation models — all open. Qwen Code, its Apache 2.0 terminal agent, points at OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini, Qwen or a local Ollama/vLLM endpoint, and states it doesn't train on your prompts or code. On coding, the 27B I ship measures 0.866 on HumanEval and about 45% (18/40) on a blind slice of SWE-bench Verified.

On language coverage I'll be honest rather than useful: I have no sourced, apples-to-apples multilingual evaluation of these two families, so I won't invent one. Both are developed in China with Chinese and English as first-class targets, and Qwen extends into speech via open ASR and TTS. If multilingual quality decides it, test both on your own languages.

Where each family genuinely wins

DeepSeek wins on license simplicity at the frontier. Releasing a 1.6T-parameter model under MIT is genuinely unusual. Nobody else at that scale hands over weights with a license that short.

DeepSeek wins on hosted price and on the training opt-out. It documents a real control — an in-product "Improve the model for everyone" toggle plus an email opt-out — and its terms assign output rights to you. Qwen documents no equivalent toggle; it trains on de-identified User Content and Feedback on a legitimate-interests basis, offering only generic rights to object or withdraw consent via a DPO email.

Qwen wins on the size ladder and on shipped software. Eight open sizes is a real choice, not one take-it-or-leave-it checkpoint, and Qwen Studio ships native clients on Web, iOS, Android, macOS and Windows. As of mid-2026 DeepSeek's App Store listing covers iPhone and iPad only and its downloads URL returns a 404; I couldn't confirm a macOS client either way from a primary source.

Both win for the wider ecosystem. DeepSeek's DeepGEMM, DeepEP, FlashMLA, 3FS and DeepSeek-OCR are MIT, as is Qwen's FlashQLA.

Who should pick which

Pick DeepSeek if you run server-side inference at volume and price per token dominates, if you want frontier-scale open weights and have the hardware, or if a documented training opt-out is something your compliance team checks for.

Pick Qwen if you need a model that fits consumer hardware, if Apache 2.0's patent grant matters to your legal review, if you want open multimodal or speech models rather than text alone, or if you want a desktop client today.

Pick neither hosted service if the data can't leave your jurisdiction. DeepSeek stores personal data in the PRC; Qwen stores and processes it in Singapore and Mainland China. Running the weights yourself is the only version where that question doesn't arise — which is why Outlier exists, and why it's Qwen weights inside it. Outlier also has no web search, no image generation, no mobile app and no Windows or Linux build.
