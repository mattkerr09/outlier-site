The best AI assistant for Mac in 2026: my picks

By Matt Kerr · June 20, 2026 Updated 2026-10-19 ChatGPT, Claude, Google AI Plus, Microsoft 365, Apple Intelligence, LM Studio, Ollama and Jan prices and plans checked 2026-10-19
Disclosure: I'm Matt Kerr, the founder of Outlier, one of the tools in this roundup. I've done my best to be fair, and I'll call out where cloud tools have real advantages. Where Outlier has an honest edge, I'll say so and explain why. All tools were run on my own Mac hardware; the ratings are my own judgments, not paid placements.
Quick answer

For widest ecosystem and capabilities (image generation, plugins, voice, web search), ChatGPT is hard to beat. For long-context reasoning and writing, Claude is excellent. If you live in Microsoft 365, Copilot fits naturally. For privacy, offline use, and no usage caps on an Apple Silicon Mac, Outlier is the one to try. Cloud models like Claude and ChatGPT are faster and stronger on the hardest work; Outlier keeps all your data on your Mac, offline and unmetered.

Mac users in 2026 have more AI options than ever: cloud tools with massive model quality, local tools that never leave your machine, and Apple's own built-in intelligence. The right choice depends on what you actually need. Here's how they stack up.

Cloud AI assistants for Mac

Cloud tools run models that are larger than anything you can fit locally today. That gap has been narrowing, but it's still real for the most demanding tasks. The tradeoffs are consistent: your data goes to a server, you need an internet connection, and free tiers impose usage limits.

ChatGPT

OpenAI's ChatGPT remains the broadest ecosystem play. It handles text, images, voice, and code in a single interface. Its plugin and GPT store adds breadth few others match: you can connect it to web search, image generation, code interpreter, and a long list of third-party integrations. The Mac app is solid, not fully native, but genuinely polished. The plans are Free at $0, Go at $8, Plus at $20 and Pro from $100 per month (OpenAI's pricing page, read in a browser on 2026-10-19). Which models and how much usage each plan includes is on that page and changes, so I'm not listing it here. Data goes to OpenAI's servers.

Claude

Anthropic's Claude (Sonnet and Opus models) consistently impresses on reasoning, writing, and long-document work. Its long context window is genuinely useful for processing large codebases or long PDFs. The Mac app and web experience are clean and reliable. Pro is $20/month ($17 a month billed annually). Every Claude plan has usage limits, Free included, and Pro gives you more usage than Free. Claude is particularly strong if your main use cases are drafting, research synthesis, and nuanced analysis. Data is sent to Anthropic's servers.

Google AI Plus

Google's AI Plus plan ($4.99/month in the US) makes most sense if you're already deep in Google Workspace: Docs, Drive, Gmail integration is real and useful. Raw model quality is competitive.

Copilot

Microsoft's Copilot has a free tier with limited usage on a free Microsoft account; the paid features ride on a Microsoft 365 subscription, and Microsoft's plan comparison lists no standalone Copilot Pro plan. The value proposition is largely about Microsoft 365 integration: it can draft in Word, summarize in Outlook, and generate in PowerPoint. If you use those apps heavily, Copilot earns its keep. Microsoft says Microsoft 365 Personal works on PCs, Macs, phones and tablets, and that features vary by platform. Requires internet; data goes to Microsoft.

Apple Intelligence

Apple's built-in AI layer (macOS 15+, M-series Macs) is free and deeply integrated into the OS: summarizing notifications, rewriting text in any app, generating images in Messages. It's useful precisely because it requires no extra app or subscription and reads your existing mail, messages and notes without setup. The capability ceiling is lower than a dedicated AI assistant: it handles everyday polish and summarization well, but deep research or extended reasoning isn't its job. No subscription, no data leaving the device for on-device tasks.

Local AI options for Mac

Local AI runs the model entirely on your Mac: nothing leaves your machine, no internet required, no usage caps. The tradeoff is speed and ceiling: cloud models like Claude and ChatGPT are faster and stronger on the hardest work.

Outlier

Outlier is a native Mac app (Apple Silicon only) built specifically for local AI. It offers six model tiers from Nano 4B up through Core 27B, with a free tier that includes Nano and Lite (9B): useful for quick queries and offline note-taking. The Pro plan is a one-time $249 and unlocks all tiers including Core 27B.

Core 27B is the one to pay attention to. We published a 54-prompt side-by-side of it and Claude Opus on everyday tasks (writing, summarization, Q&A, coding) so you can compare the answers yourself. See the side-by-side here.

Beyond chat, Outlier includes deep research (multi-step web search and synthesis), coding agents that can read and edit your actual codebase, and vision support. Everything processes on-device. There's no account required for the free tier.

LM Studio

LM Studio is a free, cross-platform desktop app with a well-organized model library and a decent enough chat interface. It's a strong choice for experimenting with a wide range of models from Hugging Face, the model browser and one-click download flow are genuinely good. Its free tier now includes Bionic, LM Studio's own agent for work and code, and it sells optional hosted plans on top (Bionic+ at $20 a month, Pro at $100). What it lacks: native Mac-first design choices. A solid option for model exploration; less suited as a daily AI assistant.

Ollama

Ollama is a CLI tool that makes running local models via terminal straightforward, and it exposes a local OpenAI-compatible API that other apps can connect to. It's free to run locally, and it also sells cloud plans for hosted models (Pro at $20 a month, Max at $100, Team at $500). It's excellent for developers who want to integrate local models into their own scripts or tools. If you want to type ollama run llama3 and get a response, it works. It's built around the terminal and the API, not deep research or Mac app polish, and for API automation and developer workflows, it's a pragmatic choice.

Jan

Jan is open-source and cross-platform with an improving basic chat UI. It's genuinely getting better with each release, and the open-source ethos appeals to developers who want full transparency. It's still catching up on Mac-specific polish and integrated features. Worth watching; not yet the daily-driver choice for most Mac users.

How to choose

The right tool genuinely depends on what you need most:

  • Privacy first, data never leaves your Mac: Outlier (free tier to start, Pro for the full model range). Apple Intelligence also qualifies for OS-level tasks.
  • Widest ecosystem: plugins, voice, image generation: ChatGPT. Nothing else comes close for sheer breadth of what you can bolt on.
  • Long documents, deep reasoning, nuanced writing: Claude. The long context window and quality on extended tasks is genuinely strong.
  • Microsoft 365 user: Copilot. The integration payoff only materializes if you actually live in Word, Outlook, and Teams.
  • Google Workspace user: Google AI Plus. Same logic: integration is the differentiator.
  • Free local AI, just getting started: Outlier's free tier (Nano 4B + Lite 9B) is the lowest-friction entry point: it's a real Mac app, no account needed.
  • Developer who wants an API or CLI: Ollama for simplicity; LM Studio if you want a GUI for model management alongside the API.
  • Zero subscription, zero fuss: Apple Intelligence for light tasks; Outlier free tier for anything heavier.

The case for using both

Many people end up running a cloud tool and a local tool side-by-side, and that's a reasonable setup. Use ChatGPT or Claude for tasks that genuinely need frontier-scale reasoning: complex multi-step analysis, image generation, or things you want to throw at the most capable model available. Use a local tool like Outlier for the routine, high-frequency stuff: drafting, summarizing a document, answering questions about your codebase, anything you'd rather not send off-device.

The combination also covers you when your internet connection fails, when you're traveling, or when usage caps hit at an inconvenient moment. Running both costs nothing extra if you start with free tiers.
