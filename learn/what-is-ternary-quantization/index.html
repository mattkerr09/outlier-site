What is ternary quantization (and what it isn't)

Outlier · last updated 2026-05-19

Ternary quantization is a research-backed compression technique that stores each model weight as one of three values: -1, 0, or +1. The promise is dramatic — about 16× smaller than 16-bit floats, vs 4× for the standard 4-bit quantization most local-AI tools use. The reality in 2026 is more nuanced: ternary works for specific model families trained with it in mind, but it hasn't replaced 4-bit as the production default.

How quantization works in one paragraph

Most language model weights are stored as 16-bit floating point numbers (bf16 or fp16). Quantization replaces those with lower-precision integers plus a small scaling factor per group. 8-bit halves the memory; 4-bit quarters it; 2-bit cuts it 8×; ternary (1.58 bits per weight, since you need 3 states) compresses about 10× smaller than 16-bit. Lower precision means smaller model files and lower memory bandwidth requirements during inference — both of which matter for local AI.

What ternary specifically is

Ternary quantization (BitNet b1.58 is the canonical 2024 paper) stores each weight as -1, 0, or +1. With 3 states, you need log₂(3) ≈ 1.58 bits per weight in theory. In practice you'd pack 5 ternary weights into 8 bits (since 3⁵ = 243 fits in a byte) for ~1.6 bits per weight effective.

The arithmetic gets simpler too: multiplying by -1, 0, or +1 is sign-flip, zero-out, or pass-through. No multiplication hardware needed for the weight side of the dot product. That's the second promised win: cheaper matmul.

What the catches are

Two big ones. First: ternary works best on models trained with ternary in mind (quantization-aware training, or QAT). Post-training quantization from a pretrained 16-bit model down to ternary loses meaningful quality — the model wasn't optimized to be representable in 1.58 bits. You can do it but the output quality regresses noticeably.

Second: tooling and hardware support. 4-bit quantization is supported by MLX, llama.cpp, vLLM, every major inference framework. Ternary needs custom kernels because the standard matmul isn't optimized for {-1, 0, +1}. As of 2026 the production tooling for ternary is still catching up; most ternary models you see in the wild are research demos rather than shipped products.

Where 4-bit sits in 2026

4-bit is the production sweet spot. Quality regression vs 16-bit is small enough to be invisible on most tasks. Tooling is mature. Hardware (Apple Silicon Metal, NVIDIA Tensor Cores, AMD ROCm) all support 4-bit matmul natively. Outlier ships every tier in MLX 4-bit:

  • Nano 4B → ~3 GB
  • Lite 9B → ~6 GB
  • Quick 26B → ~16 GB
  • Core 27B → ~16 GB
  • Vision 35B-A3B → ~20 GB
  • Plus 397B-A17B → ~209 GB

At 4-bit, Core 27B's HumanEval pass@1 is 0.8659 — within rounding noise of the 16-bit base model. The quality cost was effectively zero; the size savings were 4×.

When ternary might matter

Ternary becomes interesting in two scenarios:

  • Mobile / battery-constrained. If you're trying to run a 7B model on a phone with 6 GB RAM and 5W of power budget, ternary's smaller bandwidth requirement saves both memory and energy. This is where research is concentrated.
  • Specialized accelerators. If you have hardware designed for ternary operations (no commercial example yet, but research chips exist), the speedup over 4-bit is significant.

For a Mac in 2026, neither of those applies. The unified-memory architecture and Metal-optimized 4-bit kernels make 4-bit the right pick for current and near-future hardware.

The short answer

Ternary quantization is a real research direction that may matter in the next 2–3 years as mobile and edge accelerators mature. It is not a free lunch you can apply to existing pretrained models to make them 4× smaller than 4-bit without quality loss. For local AI on a Mac today, 4-bit is the right format — which is what Outlier and most other Mac-native local-AI tools ship.
